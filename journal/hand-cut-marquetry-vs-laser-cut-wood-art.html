Hand-Cut Marquetry or Laser-Cut Wood Art? How to Tell the Difference

By Sasun Sargsyan · 25 November 2026 · 3 min read

Search for a wooden portrait online and you will find thousands, at every price. Most are made by machine, and the photographs rarely tell you so. This guide explains the four main kinds of “wood art”, how to recognise each one, and what to ask before you buy or commission.

Four kinds of wood art

1. Laser-engraved

A laser burns the image into the surface of a single board, the way a printer lays down dots. The result is monochrome, from light tan to black, and the grain of one board runs straight across the whole picture.

2. Printed on wood

A photograph is printed with ink, usually UV-cured, onto a wooden panel. It can be full colour and very detailed, but the image sits on the wood rather than being made of it.

3. Laser-cut marquetry

Real veneer pieces are cut by a laser from a digital file and assembled. It is genuine wood, but the cutting is done by a machine, and the same file can be cut again and again.

4. Hand-cut marquetry

Every piece of veneer is cut by hand, with a knife or a fine saw, and fitted to its neighbours by eye. Each piece is unique; it cannot be repeated exactly even by the person who made it.

Five signs to look for

  1. Dark outlines along every edge. A laser cuts by burning, which leaves a thin charred line of uniform width around each piece. Hand-cut seams are clean, with the natural colour of the wood right up to the joint.
  2. One board, one grain. If the grain runs uninterrupted across a face, the background and the clothing alike, the image was engraved or printed on a single board.
  3. The grain changes with the form. In hand-cut marquetry, the grain of each piece is chosen to describe what it shows: running down a strand of hair, curving around a cheek.
  4. Colour that only wood could have. A print can reproduce any colour, including blues and greens that rarely occur in wood. Natural marquetry has the palette of trees.
  5. A surface you can feel. Up close, and with permission, you can often feel the slightest change in texture between species. A print is perfectly uniform.

A note on honest exceptions

Not every technique that alters wood is a shortcut. Dyed veneers and hot-sand shading, in which pieces are scorched in heated sand to suggest volume, are traditional marquetry methods, and many fine makers use them. What matters is not whether a technique is used, but whether the seller tells you clearly.

Questions to ask before you commission

  • Is each piece cut by hand, or by laser or CNC?
  • How many pieces will the portrait contain?
  • Which wood species will be used?
  • Is any colour painted, dyed, stained or printed?
  • Will I see a preview before cutting begins, and progress photographs during the work?
  • Does the work come with a signed Certificate of Authenticity?

A maker who works by hand will be glad to answer every one.
