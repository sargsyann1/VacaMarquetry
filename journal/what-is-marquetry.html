What Is Marquetry? A Short Guide to Pictures Made of Wood

By Sasun Sargsyan · 25 November 2026 · 4 min read

Marquetry is the art of making pictures and patterns from thin sheets of wood. Pieces of veneer, each less than a millimetre thick, are cut to shape, fitted together like a jigsaw with no gaps, and glued to a solid backing. Every colour in the finished image is the natural colour of a different wood.

The word comes from the French marqueterie, from marqueter, “to variegate”: to make something many-coloured. That is exactly what the craft does, using nothing but the palette that trees provide.

Marquetry, inlay, intarsia, parquetry: what is the difference?

The terms are often used loosely, but they describe different techniques:

  • Inlay sets pieces of one material into recesses cut into a solid surface, such as a line of boxwood let into an oak table.
  • Intarsia is the Italian name for pictorial woodwork that flourished in the Renaissance. It began as inlay into solid wood and moved towards veneer over time; in Italian, intarsio is still the everyday word for the whole family of techniques.
  • Marquetry assembles a complete picture from veneer pieces first, as a single sheet, and then glues the whole sheet to its backing.
  • Parquetry uses the same method for geometric patterns, such as cubes, lozenges and herringbone, rather than pictures. Parquet floors take their name from it.

A short history

Decorating wood with other woods is ancient: furniture from Egyptian tombs, including Tutankhamun’s, is inlaid with ebony, ivory and precious materials. But pictures made of wood reached their first great height in Renaissance Italy.

In the fifteenth century, Italian craftsmen used intarsia to create astonishing illusions of depth. The most famous example is the studiolo of Federico da Montefeltro in the Ducal Palace of Urbino, completed in the 1470s: a small study whose walls appear to hold half-open cupboards, books, instruments and armour, all of it flat wood. A second studiolo, made for his palace in Gubbio, is now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

As veneer saws improved, the technique spread north. In the late seventeenth century, André-Charles Boulle, cabinetmaker to Louis XIV, became so identified with marquetry of brass and tortoiseshell that it still carries his name. In the eighteenth century, French and German masters such as Jean-Henri Riesener and David Roentgen produced pictorial marquetry of remarkable subtlety for the courts of Europe.

Today, the craft survives in a small number of workshops, and in museums. Its rarity is part of its appeal: there are very few people still making pictures this way.

How a marquetry picture is made

Every workshop has its own methods, but the essential stages are the same.

  1. The design. An image is translated into shapes, with a decision for every area: which wood, and which way the grain runs.
  2. Choosing the veneers. Each species brings its own tone and figure: pale maple and ash for light, oak for texture, walnut and wenge for depth and shadow.
  3. Cutting. Pieces are cut with a fine saw or, as in our studio, freehand with a knife, each one fitted against the edge of its neighbour.
  4. Assembly and gluing. The completed sheet is glued and pressed onto a stable backing panel.
  5. Finishing. The surface is sanded flat and sealed, which deepens the colours and protects the wood.

Marquetry portraits today

Portraiture is the most demanding form of marquetry. A face leaves no room for approximation: a millimetre at the corner of an eye changes the expression. A portrait may need several hundred individually cut pieces, sometimes more than nine hundred, and the craftsman has to find, in real wood, every tone the face requires, without paint to correct a choice.

That is what we do at VaCa Marquetry. Each portrait is cut freehand by the artist, Sasun Sargsyan, from natural veneers, in a studio in Pisa, Tuscany. You can see the stages of the work on The Craft, browse finished pieces in the Collection, or commission a portrait from your own photograph.

In short

  • Marquetry is a picture made of fitted pieces of wood veneer, glued to a backing.
  • It differs from inlay, which is set into solid wood, and from parquetry, which is geometric.
  • It flourished in Renaissance Italy and at the French court, and survives today in very few hands.
  • In a true marquetry portrait, every colour is the colour of the wood itself.
